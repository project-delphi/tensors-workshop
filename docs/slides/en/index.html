Map of factorizations: six matrix and tensor factorizations arranged on a ladder from LU through Cholesky, QR, SVD, Tucker and CP, each with one phrase describing what it buys.

Prime factorization is unique up to ordering — the cleanest case on the slide. Generic low-rank matrix factors have gauge freedom (the \(M\)/\(M^{-\top}\) pair); extra structure or a convention — positive pivots, orthonormal columns, a positive diagonal — is what restores uniqueness for LU, QR or Cholesky. CP can be essentially unique under suitable identifiability conditions, up to permutation and a compensating nonzero scaling across modes; over the reals, sign flips are a special case of that scaling. Kruskal’s \(k_A + k_B + k_C \ge 2R + 2\) is a sufficient condition, not a necessary one, and it says nothing about whether the decomposition is easy to find. Worth saying if there is time: computing tensor rank is NP-hard in general, tensor rank can exceed every individual mode dimension, and a best rank-\(R\) approximation need not even exist — none of that is true of matrices.

What does a factorization give you? A four-step diagram — number, polynomial, matrix, tensor — shows that factorizing reveals useful structure rather than just rewriting an expression.
